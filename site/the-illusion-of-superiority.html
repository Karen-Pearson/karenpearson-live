The Illusion of Superiority

August 30, 2026 · Karen Pearson

I'm sixteen. Amy and I sit in the last pew of the children's section, an enclosed sound-proof glass room with a panoramic view of the entire congregation. It's noon mass. We are painting our nails and trading derogatory commentary.

I feel superior to what I label "the Ignorants" - the throngs of people sucking up a sermon of hate like Slurpees from the 7-11 clerk come priest. I lean toward her and proclaim "I don't believe in god" partly for shock but mostly for truth, ascertaining my rejection distanced me above the fray. She nods and laughs in what I assume, of course, is agreement. After all, my identity as an intellectual depends on it. I knew better, or at least that's what I told myself. And I was too smart to disagree with me. But my name calling always felt a little off, slightly to the right, and down, as if I had culled an idea from the hate-sermon scraps, marketed and sold myself a belief that was simply re-branded hate. My assumptions of superiority were identical to the hate sermon. The priest asserted from above; I rejected from above. We both diminished in order to stabilize our positions.

I wasn't an innocent intellectual, I was a full on participant.

I always questioned this thing called identity, at sixteen, of being better than...but better than what? Wasn't my calling others ignorant the same as the priest calling me a sinner? Weren't we both blaming through comparing, criticizing, and complaining? While high school curriculum is focused on narrowing the mind, the environment itself is rich with the opposing ideas that create and nurture expansion. Another paradox of truth.

What did the priest know?

What did I know?

Superiority is a relationship construct, fundamentally dependent on diminishing others. It is not intrinsic. It is not sovereign. And can only be sustained under comparative conditions. Under such conditions, judgment functions as compensation for loss of heirarchy, reintroducing hierarchy to erase all traces of sovereignty. Seasons illuminate the foundational balance of the Self. Particularly, the solstices and equinoxes. They reveal what was already present but less noticed under lower "light" vibrations of daily survival, ubiquitous in high school. In that sense, negativity toward others can be understood as resistance to internal inconsistency. The mind attempts to blurr contrast when illumination makes distinctions feel unstable. In that back pew, I felt unstable.

Negativity toward others, mine included, thrives without scrutiny, without the shedding of light on inconsistent beliefs - such as honest mistakes are our own, while careless mistakes are the mistakes of others. I wrestled with my own contradictions at the level of a world war. I was no better, but also, no worse than the priest, than any one.

Live deliberately.
